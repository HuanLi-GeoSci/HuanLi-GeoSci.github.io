Huan Li

I study rain and clouds from space and from the ground: how satellites estimate precipitation, where those estimates fail, and what radars can tell us about the light rain they miss.

Remote sensing scientist, Washington, DC area

My doctoral and postdoctoral research merged satellite precipitation estimates with rain gauges using variational analysis. From 2018 to 2023, at the Earth System Science Interdisciplinary Center of the University of Maryland, I worked on warm rain and marine boundary-layer clouds with ground-based cloud radar, CloudSat, MODIS and VIIRS. My current work uses EarthCARE and GPM radar observations of drizzle and warm rain. I hold a Ph.D. in remote sensing from the Chinese Academy of Sciences and a B.S. in computer science.

Research

Marine drizzle from EarthCARE and the ARM Eastern North Atlantic site

2024 to present

I match EarthCARE cloud-radar overpasses of the ARM Eastern North Atlantic site, 201 between December 2024 and May 2026, with the ground-based KAZR radar, 1,585 radiosondes and ERA5. The aim is to check what the spaceborne radar sees against ground references, and to follow marine drizzle from its onset in cloud to its evaporation below cloud base under different weather regimes.

A companion analysis pairs GPM DPR with EarthCARE to measure how much warm rain falls below the DPR sensitivity floor. I am also developing a retrieval of air motion and drop fall speed from the EarthCARE Doppler measurements.

First-author manuscript in preparation.

Variational merging of gauge and satellite precipitation

2012 to 2018, Chinese Academy of Sciences and University of Oklahoma

An hourly, 10 km precipitation analysis over mainland China from China Meteorological Administration gauges and NOAA CMORPH. I developed and implemented the scheme: a cost function with a recursive filter that spreads observations to nearby grid points, a gradient derived with the adjoint technique, and quasi-Newton minimization.

Cross-validation against withheld gauges showed that the analysis improves rainfall estimates over CMORPH and bias-adjusted CMORPH, particularly in data-sparse regions. I continued the work as principal investigator of National Natural Science Foundation of China grant 41501471.

Li et al. (2015), Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres, doi:10.1002/2015JD023710. With Y. Hong, P. Xie (NOAA CPC), J. Gao (NOAA NSSL), Z. Niu, P. Kirstetter and B. Yong.

Water vapor from SEVIRI infrared channels

2017, CIMSS, University of Wisconsin–Madison

I worked on adapting the GOES Sounder atmospheric profile algorithm to the SEVIRI imager to retrieve precipitable water vapor. The retrieval has two stages: a nonlinear regression trained on a large database of atmospheric profiles, then a physical retrieval that uses the PFAAST radiative transfer model.

Modeled and observed brightness temperatures differ slightly, so the work included a bias correction for five infrared bands derived from matched profiles.

Supported by China Scholarship Council award 201704910117 (principal investigator).

Warm rain and marine boundary-layer clouds

2018 to 2023, ESSIC, University of Maryland

How much of the drizzle that forms in marine stratocumulus evaporates, and how much reaches the surface? Using ARM ground-based cloud radar, I examined how often these clouds drizzle and how drizzle scales with cloud thickness, within the evaporation and sedimentation framework that CloudSat rain retrievals also use.

Related work covered warm-rain processes from CloudSat and MODIS, and a warm-rain retrieval from VIIRS cloud properties on Suomi-NPP and NOAA-20. I also contributed to the EAST-AIRCPC synthesis on aerosols, clouds and precipitation (Li et al., 2019).

Gauge-based daily precipitation analysis over China

2010 to 2013, Chinese Academy of Sciences

I worked on a daily precipitation analysis on a 0.25° grid for 1951 to 2010 from 2,419 quality-controlled gauges, built by optimal interpolation around a climatology adjusted with PRISM to correct orographic bias.

The analysis is reliable over eastern China, where stations are dense, and less so in the west, where values are interpolated from distant stations. That limit is what motivated adding satellite estimates in the variational work above.
